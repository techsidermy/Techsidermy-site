The practice

Natural history,
for machines.

§ 01

For as long as we've had museums, we've preserved the things we were afraid to forget. Birds pinned beneath glass, skeletons wired upright, specimens labeled in careful handwriting. Not to keep them alive, but to keep looking at them.

Biology will always be studied. But something new has started to pile up alongside it: the machines that shaped how we grew up. Phones that held entire chapters of a life. Handhelds played under the covers. Music players that went everywhere with us. They don't decay. They just go quiet, and then they go into a drawer.

Techsidermy is a practice of preserving them properly. Each device is taken apart by hand, every component catalogued, then mounted at true 1:1 scale above a plate drawn like a circuit board, numbered and behind glass. It's half natural history museum and half gallery: rigorous enough to learn from and composed enough to live with.

Every specimen gets a number, a record and, when it comes from a client, a single line in their own words. The number is permanent. The Archive keeps growing.

§ 02
Method

Three disciplines.

I · Dissect

Down to the last screw

Disassembled by hand with the right tools, in order, without forcing. Nothing is discarded; even the screws are kept and counted.

II · Catalogue

Every part, recorded

Each component is measured, numbered and named on a plate drawn for that specific device at true 1:1 scale, with traces tucked beneath every part.

III · Preserve

Behind glass, for good

Mounted on clear standoffs in a white wood shadowbox under UV-filtering acrylic. Batteries are discharged and sealed. Made to outlast the device's first life.
